Other CAWG assertions and the big picture

Provenance isn’t only about the past. Creators also want to say what may happen to their work next. This chapter covers the AI-usage assertion, then steps back to weigh what the whole system can and can’t do.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to

  • Build and read a cawg.training-mining assertion.
  • Explain the privacy design choices in C2PA and CAWG.
  • Give a balanced account of what Content Credentials can and cannot establish.

Training and data mining: cawg.training-mining

Many creators want a say in whether their work is used to train AI models. The CAWG Training and Data Mining Assertion (version 1.1, DIF ratified, May 2025) gives them a standard, machine-readable way to state their preferences inside the signed manifest. (C2PA 1.x had a similar c2pa.training-mining assertion. CAWG’s version uses the cawg. prefix because it isn’t a C2PA product.)

The assertion holds an entries map. Four keys are predefined:

  • cawg.data_mining: extracting patterns or trends, including text recognition (OCR) in images.
  • cawg.ai_inference: using the asset as input to a trained model to get a result (for example, asking a model to describe it).
  • cawg.ai_training: training non-generative models, such as classifiers or object detectors.
  • cawg.ai_generative_training: training models that can generate new content.

Each entry has a use value: allowed, notAllowed or constrained. With constrained, an optional constraint_info string explains the conditions, such as a licence name or a URL to a policy. If a use is constrained but no information is given, consumers should treat it as notAllowed. Custom entry keys are allowed if they follow C2PA’s custom-label rules.

A preference, not a lock

The assertion is cryptographically bound to the asset and signed, so nobody can quietly change your stated preference. But it can’t technically prevent anyone from training on your image. Its force comes from how AI developers, platforms, contracts and regulations choose to honour machine-readable opt-outs. Pairing it with an identity assertion lets the preference be attributed to the rights holder.

Metadata: cawg.metadata

C2PA 2.x restricted its own c2pa.metadata assertion to a fixed set of fields. CAWG’s metadata assertion fills the gap. It carries richer descriptive metadata (captions, keywords, creator information from IPTC, Dublin Core, EXIF and others) as JSON-LD inside the manifest, where it gains the integrity protection that ordinary file metadata lacks (chapter 1).

Privacy by design

Provenance can expose people. Several design choices try to limit that:

  • Identity is optional. C2PA signatures identify products, not people. Adding a CAWG identity assertion is the creator’s choice.
  • Selective vouching. A credential holder chooses which assertions they reference. A photographer can sign their creative role without endorsing an embedded location.
  • Redaction. Sensitive assertions, such as GPS location, can be removed from earlier manifests in a transparent, accountable way (chapter 9).
  • Minimal data to third parties. Time-stamp authorities see only a hash (chapter 6).

Risks remain. A camera that always signs with a unique per-device certificate could, in principle, link all photos from one device. Viewers and platforms must present provenance responsibly. And the absence of credentials must never be treated as proof of fakery, or people who strip metadata to protect themselves would be penalised.

What Content Credentials can establish

  • This exact content (byte-for-byte, or via soft binding a close relative) is described by this manifest.
  • The manifest hasn’t changed since it was signed.
  • It was signed by a product whose certificate chains to a CA on your validator’s trust list, at a time vouched for by a trusted TSA.
  • The product recorded how the content was made (captured, generated, composited) and what was done to it, with the same guarantees for each ingredient whose manifest is present.
  • With CAWG: a named person or organisation, verified by an issuer you trust, cryptographically associated themselves with the content in a stated role, and stated their AI-usage preferences.

What they cannot establish

  • Truth. A real camera can photograph a staged scene. Provenance shows origin, not reality.
  • Honesty of signers. A trusted product with a bug, or a compromised key, can sign false assertions. Trust lists and revocation limit the damage but can’t make it zero.
  • Completeness. Steps taken outside C2PA-aware tools leave gaps. An ingredient without credentials has unknown provenance.
  • Anything about unsigned content. Most content today has no credentials, and stripping is easy. “No credentials” means “no information”.
  • Universal trust. “Trusted” is always relative to a validator’s trust configuration, a policy choice made by whoever builds the viewer.

The ecosystem

The system only works if many parties take part. Camera makers sign at capture. Editing tools preserve and extend manifests. AI services label generated output. Platforms keep credentials and show them to viewers. News organisations add identity and publication records. Governments and regulators increasingly point to provenance standards in rules on AI transparency. Each participant is a claim generator or validator in the vocabulary you now know.
