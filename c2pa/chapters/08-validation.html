Validation and the Tamper Lab

A manifest is only as useful as the checks a validator runs on it. Here you’ll learn those checks, then play the attacker and watch each one catch you.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to

  • List the main steps of C2PA validation and the status codes they produce.
  • Distinguish well-formed, valid and trusted manifests, and a manifest that matches its content.
  • Predict which check fails for a given attack.

What a validator does

A C2PA validator, the code behind a “Verify” website or the “cr” pin in an app, performs roughly these steps on the active manifest:

  1. Locate and parse the manifest store, and identify the active manifest. Parsing failures produce codes such as claim.malformed or manifest.inaccessible.
  2. Validate the claim signature. Rebuild the to-be-signed bytes and verify the COSE signature with the public key in the leaf certificate → claimSignature.validated or claimSignature.mismatch.
  3. Validate the time-stamp, if present → timeStamp.validated, timeStamp.trusted, timeStamp.mismatch…, and establish the signing time.
  4. Validate the signing credential: certificate profile, validity at the signing time (claimSignature.insideValidity/outsideValidity), revocation (signingCredential.ocsp.revoked…), and the chain to a trust anchor (signingCredential.trusted or signingCredential.untrusted).
  5. Validate every assertion reference: each hashed URI in the claim must resolve to an assertion whose hash matches (assertion.hashedURI.match / mismatch, assertion.missing). Assertions in the store but not in the claim are flagged (assertion.undeclared).
  6. Validate the ingredients: check each ingredient’s manifest in turn (chapter 9).
  7. Validate the content: recompute the hard-binding hash over the asset → assertion.dataHash.match / mismatch (or the bmff/boxes equivalents). A manifest with no hard binding gets claim.hardBindings.missing.

Each result is reported as a success, informational or failure status code, with a URL pointing at what it refers to.

Three states, plus one

Since version 2.1, the specification summarises a manifest’s validation as one of these states:

StateMeans
Well-formedIts structure obeys the spec: the right assertions are present and correctly formed, and ingredients meet their requirements.
ValidWell-formed, and not modified since signing: the claim signature validates, the certificate was valid at signing time, it wasn’t revoked, and all hashed URIs match. A valid manifest’s claim “can be attributed to the claim generator” named in it.
TrustedValid, and the signing credential chains to a trust anchor the validator accepts (for example, on the C2PA Trust List).

Separately, the specification talks about a valid asset: the content matches the hard binding. A manifest can be perfectly trusted while describing different content, for example when someone copies a good manifest onto a different image. A complete answer needs both.

Validators don’t judge

The specification states that validators shouldn’t make value judgements about whether provenance is “good” or “bad”. A trusted manifest saying “generated by AI” is just as trusted as one saying “captured by a camera”. Validation is about integrity and attribution, not approval.

Notice the pattern. Every attack that modifies something is caught by some link in the chain. The two remaining weaknesses are trust policy (a validator that trusts the wrong roots will be fooled by the re-signing attack) and removal (an attacker can always strip the credentials and hope nobody asks why they are missing). The next chapter looks at what C2PA does about removal.

Simplifications in this lab

The real validation algorithm has more steps and codes, such as algorithm checks, certificate profile checks, ingredient deltas, and update manifests. Real validators may also continue after the first failure and report several. The codes shown are taken from the C2PA 2.x specification, and the lab reproduces its logic faithfully for the checks it covers.
