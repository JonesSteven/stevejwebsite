Why identity needed its own spec

A C2PA signature tells you which product made a manifest. It doesn’t tell you which person or organisation stands behind the content. CAWG exists to fill that gap.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to

  • Explain the difference between the claim generator and the person using it.
  • Explain why C2PA 2.0 moved human identity out of the core specification.
  • Describe what CAWG is, who runs it, and what it publishes.

Who signed this, really?

Remember what the claim signature’s certificate identifies: the claim generator. When you export from an editing app, the manifest is signed with a key belonging to that app or its maker, often kept in the vendor’s cloud service. When a camera signs, the key belongs to that camera model or unit. So the signature means: “this product, operating as designed, produced this manifest.”

For many purposes that is exactly right. Knowing that an image came straight from a C2PA-conformant camera, or from a particular AI service, is valuable. But it leaves out something people care about: who made it, and in what role.

Why not just put people in C2PA certificates?

Early versions of C2PA (1.x) tried to describe human “actors” and included a section on W3C Verifiable Credentials. Version 2.0, in January 2024, made what its own changelog calls a significant departure. It reduced the use of “actor”, which no longer represents humans and organisations; introduced the C2PA Trust List for hardware and software; required X.509 certificates; and removed the Verifiable Credentials section. Reasons for that separation include:

  • Different trust problems. Deciding whether a camera model or app is conformant is a product-certification question. Deciding whether someone is really “Jane Photographer of Reuters” is an identity-verification question. Mixing them confuses both.
  • Privacy and safety. Identity must be optional. Many legitimate creators, such as whistleblowers, activists and people at risk, need to publish authentic content without revealing who they are. Provenance about the device or tool should not force identity disclosure.
  • Diverse identity systems. People prove who they are in many ways: corporate certificates, verified social accounts, government ID checks, decentralised identifiers. A content-provenance spec shouldn’t have to standardise all of them.

Enter CAWG

The Creator Assertions Working Group (CAWG) began in 2024, incubated within the Content Authenticity Initiative community, and is now a working group of the Decentralized Identity Foundation (DIF). Its specifications are published as DIF ratified specifications at cawg.io, and they define assertions: data structures that live inside C2PA manifests, alongside C2PA’s own. Because they are ordinary assertions, they get all of C2PA’s integrity protection for free. They are hashed into the claim and covered by the claim signature.

CAWG’s main specifications are:

SpecificationLabelPurpose
Identity Assertion (v1.3, Aug 2026)cawg.identityLets a named actor (a person or organisation) cryptographically bind their identity, and their role, to an asset.
Training and Data Mining Assertion (v1.1, May 2025)cawg.training-miningRecords whether the asset may be used for AI training, inference or data mining.
Metadata Assertion (v1.1)cawg.metadataCarries descriptive metadata (EXIF, IPTC, Dublin Core… as JSON-LD) that C2PA 2.x no longer allows in its restricted c2pa.metadata assertion.

CAWG also publishes an Organizational Identity Profile and user-experience guidance, and is drafting proposed extensions, including a Consent assertion, an Endorsement assertion, a certificate policy, and identity-assertion variants built on verifiable presentations and vLEI (verifiable Legal Entity Identifiers). Drafts can change significantly before ratification, so this course focuses on the ratified specifications.

cawg. is a reserved prefix: claim generators must not invent their own labels beginning with it. When you see cawg.* in a manifest, its meaning is defined by these specifications.

The mental model: two signers, two questions

C2PA claim signature

Signer: the claim generator (product).

Question answered: “Did trusted software or hardware produce this record of the asset’s origin and history, unchanged?”

Trust list: the C2PA Trust List (plus local policy).

CAWG identity assertion

Signer: the credential holder (a person or organisation).

Question answered: “Who stands behind this content, in what role, as verified by whom?”

Trust: the identity issuers the validator accepts (CAs, identity aggregators).
