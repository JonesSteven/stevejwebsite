C2PA in real products today

Which cameras, phones, editing tools, AI generators and platforms actually create, keep or show Content Credentials, and what their real-world rollouts teach about the ideas in this course.

A snapshot, not a buyer’s guide

This page reflects what we could check in October 2026. Support changes with every firmware update and app release, and many “supports C2PA” claims online are out of date or wrong. Before you rely on a product, check the official C2PA Conformance Explorer and test a real file in a validator such as Content Authenticity Inspect. Product names here are used only to describe them. No company has reviewed or endorsed this page.

Two different questions: “supports” vs “conforming”

A product can support C2PA, meaning it writes or reads manifests, without being a conforming product. Conformance is the C2PA program described in chapters 5 and 9. A product that passes it is listed publicly, and its signing certificate can chain to the official C2PA trust list. Listed generator products also get an assurance level:

  • Level 1: the product meets the spec’s requirements, and its keys are protected in ordinary ways.
  • Level 2: stronger, attested protection. The signing key is held in secure hardware, and the platform proves that the key and the app are genuine (for example Android Key Attestation or a cloud HSM attestation).

The program only started in 2025, so many earlier implementations, including every dedicated camera below, still sign with certificates from the manufacturer’s own trust list rather than the C2PA one. Validators may show those as “valid signature, unknown signer” unless the validator also trusts the manufacturer.

Dedicated cameras

Signing at the moment of capture is the strongest form of provenance: there is no earlier step for anyone to have altered. A few professional cameras do it today. Most target news photographers, and several need a paid licence or a newsroom service.

MakerModelsHow it is turned onNotes
Leica M11-P (2023), M11-D, SL3-S Built in, enabled in the menu The M11-P was the first camera to sign C2PA manifests at capture. Keys sit in a dedicated security chip. Earlier models such as the M11, Q3 and SL3 lack that hardware.
Sony Alpha 1, Alpha 1 II, Alpha 9 III, Alpha 7 IV, Alpha 7R V, Alpha 7S III, FX3, FX30, PXW-Z300, with newer bodies added since Firmware, plus a licence from Sony’s Camera Authenticity Solution Sold to news organisations, not individual photographers. Sony’s “Camera Verify” service added video checks in March 2026, and the cameras also record 3D depth information to help show the subject was a real scene, not a photo of a screen.
Canon EOS R1, EOS R5 Mark II Firmware, plus Canon’s paid Authenticity Imaging System for newsrooms Rolled out first in Europe, the Middle East and Africa. The rollout had false starts, so check Canon’s current support pages for your region.
Nikon Z6III (firmware 2.00, August 2025) Firmware, plus a certificate from Nikon Imaging Cloud Suspended. See “Revocation in the real world” below.
Fujifilm, Panasonic None shipping that we could confirm Announced Both are C2PA/CAI members and have announced plans, but we found no signing camera on sale.

As of 22 October 2026, none of these cameras appear on the C2PA conforming products list. They sign under their makers’ own certificates.

Smartphones and chips

Phones take most of the world’s photos, so this is where adoption matters most. It is also very uneven.

  • Google Pixel 10 (2025): Pixel Camera adds Content Credentials to every JPEG it takes, by default. It is a conforming product at assurance level 2. Signing keys are made and kept in the Titan M2 security chip, and Android Key Attestation proves that to Google’s certificate authority. Two details connect directly to this course:
    • One key and certificate per image. Because each photo is signed with a fresh certificate, two photos cannot be linked to each other or to the owner through the signature. That puts chapter 11’s principle into practice: device provenance should not force identity disclosure.
    • An on-device time-stamp authority. The phone keeps a secure clock and time-stamps each signature, even offline, so the credential stays verifiable after the short-lived certificate expires (chapter 6).
  • Google Photos (Android and iOS) adds a new manifest when you edit a photo that already has credentials, or use its AI editing tools. It also shows credentials in a photo’s details. This is the ingredient chain from chapter 9 in an everyday app.
  • Samsung Galaxy S25 and later (One UI 7): adds Content Credentials to images created or edited with Galaxy AI tools, not to ordinary camera photos. It labels AI involvement rather than proving capture.
  • Xiaomi and vivo: their camera apps are conforming generators at level 1, and their gallery apps are conforming validators that can show credentials.
  • Qualcomm Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5: the chip platform itself is listed as a level 2 generator. Phone makers using it still have to switch the feature on.
  • Apple iPhone: as far as we could find, the built-in Camera app does not sign C2PA manifests. iPhone users who need capture provenance use third-party camera apps. Several, such as ProofMode, ProofSnap and InReality Capture, are conforming products at level 1 and use Apple’s App Attest.

Editing, publishing and asset-management software

An editor that receives a signed file should validate it, record what it changed and sign a new manifest with the old one as an ingredient (chapter 9). These tools do some or all of that:

  • Adobe Photoshop and Lightroom: can attach Content Credentials when you export, recording the edits made and the files used. It is an opt-in setting, so most exported images still have none.
  • Adobe Content Authenticity app: a free web app for adding credentials to finished work in bulk. It can attach verified identity, such as a LinkedIn verified name or linked social accounts, which is the CAWG identity assertion from chapter 12. It can also record a preference about generative-AI training, the CAWG training and data-mining assertion from chapter 13.
  • Photo Mechanic (Camera Bits), the ingest and captioning tool many news photographers use, is a conforming generator, as is the IPTC C2PA Signer Tool for publishers.
  • Snapseed and Google Photos sign the edits they make.
  • Digital asset management and image delivery: Adobe Experience Manager Assets and Cloudinary are conforming generators. Fotoware and Sitecore are listed as conforming validators, so they can check credentials as files come in.

AI generators

By volume, this is where most Content Credentials come from today. Generative tools use the c2pa.actions assertion from chapter 7, with a digitalSourceType of trainedAlgorithmicMedia, to say “an AI made this.” Conforming generators include:

  • OpenAI (“OpenAI Media Service”): images from ChatGPT and the image API, and Sora video.
  • Google: many services, including Flow, Pixel Studio, NotebookLM, YouTube Create and the Music AI Sandbox. Google also adds its invisible SynthID watermark, a form of soft binding (chapter 9).
  • Amazon Bedrock, Runway, Luma AI, Stability AI, HeyGen, and the generative tools of Getty Images and iStock.

Adobe Firefly and Microsoft’s image tools (Designer, Bing Image Creator, Azure OpenAI) have attached credentials to AI output since 2023–2024, although they were not on the conforming list we checked.

A label is only as good as its survival

An AI generator that honestly labels its output protects nobody once someone strips the metadata, which takes one click. That is why generators increasingly pair the manifest with an invisible watermark, so the label can be recovered after stripping (soft binding, chapter 9).

Social platforms and content delivery

Platforms decide whether credentials reach viewers. They can read them, show them, keep them in the file they serve, or (most often) strip them.

PlatformWhat it does with credentials
LinkedInShows a “Cr” icon on images that arrive with credentials. Clicking it shows the source and edit history.
YouTubeReads credentials at upload. Video shot on a supporting camera, with an intact history, can get a “Captured with a camera” label. The streams viewers watch are re-encoded.
TikTokReads credentials to label AI-generated uploads automatically, alongside creators’ own labels and watermark detection.
Meta (Facebook, Instagram, Threads)Uses C2PA and IPTC signals to apply an “AI info” label, but does not show the full manifest.
Google Search“About this image” reports when credentials say an image was made or edited with AI.
Cloudflare ImagesA “Preserve Content Credentials” setting keeps existing manifests and adds a newly signed one describing each resize or format change. It is a working example of the ingredient chain in a CDN.

Most platforms still re-encode uploads and drop the manifest from the file viewers receive, even when they read it on the way in. If you download an image from social media and check it in a validator, the most likely result is still “no Content Credentials found”.

Newsrooms

News organisations were early users, because a trusted photo is their product. Public broadcasters such as CBC/Radio-Canada and Ukraine’s Suspilne run conforming signing platforms for what they publish. Others, such as the BBC and AFP, have published images with credentials and given feedback on the spec. The Sony and Canon newsroom services above exist for these customers.

Validators

  • Adobe Content Authenticity Inspect (verify.contentauthenticity.org) is a conforming validator, and the easiest way to check a file in a browser.
  • The CAI open-source SDKs (Rust, with JavaScript, Python and other bindings) and the c2patool command line are what most products above build on. Chapter 10’s inspector uses the JavaScript SDK.
  • Phone galleries (Google Photos, Samsung Gallery, Xiaomi Gallery, vivo Albums) increasingly show credentials directly.

What real deployments teach

Revocation in the real world: Nikon Z6III

In August 2025 Nikon added C2PA signing to the Z6III. Within weeks, a photographer showed that the camera’s multiple-exposure mode could combine an image that was not captured by the camera with a real shot, and the camera would sign the result as an authentic capture. Nikon suspended its authenticity service and revoked every certificate it had issued to Z6III cameras. As of our snapshot, the service had not returned.

This one incident exercises almost every idea from Part II:

  • A valid signature proves who signed, not that the content is true (chapter 4). The camera’s key genuinely signed a composited image.
  • Revocation is the remedy (chapter 5). Once a signer is known to be untrustworthy, its certificates are revoked.
  • Revocation only works if validators check it. Reporters noted that common validation tools did not check revocation by default, so revoked Z6III signatures could still show as valid. Your validator’s settings matter.
  • Time-stamps decide which side of the line a signature falls on (chapter 6). A trusted time-stamp is what lets a validator tell a signature made before revocation from one made after.

Privacy by design: per-image certificates

Pixel’s choice to sign each photo with its own key answers the worry that a device certificate becomes a tracking identifier. The photo still proves “a genuine Pixel camera captured this,” but not which Pixel. If a photographer wants to be named, that is a separate, deliberate step: a CAWG identity assertion (chapters 11–12).

Trust levels vary, and the weakest link sets them

A level 2 camera app and a level 1 web service both produce a valid manifest. A provenance chain that passes through both is only as strong as the weaker step (chapter 9). Validators and platforms are beginning to show assurance levels, and the C2PA trust list makes it possible to tell conforming signers from everyone else.

Labelling AI is ahead of proving reality

Most credentials in circulation today say “AI made this,” not “a camera captured this.” Proving capture needs hardware support in cameras and phones, and that is arriving slowly: one phone line signs every photo, a handful of professional cameras do, and the most popular phone does not. Until capture signing is common, a missing credential tells you almost nothing about whether an image is real.

How to check the current state yourself

  • C2PA Conformance Explorer, and the raw conforming products list, which shows product type and assurance level.
  • Content Authenticity Inspect: drop in a file straight from the device or app you want to test.
  • Content Authenticity Initiative news and member announcements.
