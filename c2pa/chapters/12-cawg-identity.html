The CAWG identity assertion

The identity assertion lets a person or organisation sign their own statement about an asset, bound so tightly to the C2PA manifest and content that it can’t be lifted off and reused.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to

  • Describe the structure of cawg.identity: signer_payload, signature, padding.
  • Explain referenced assertions, and why the hard binding must be one of them.
  • Explain the two credential types: X.509 certificates and identity claims aggregation (verifiable credentials).
  • Walk through validation and interpret cawg.identity.* status codes.
Spec status

Based on CAWG Identity Assertion 1.3, a DIF Ratified Specification (17 August 2026). Version 1.2 (December 2025) added a trust model for X.509 certificates. Version 1.3 removed the optional expected_partial_claim, expected_claim_generator and expected_countersigners fields, and gave X.509 validation its own cawg.x509.* status codes. You may still meet assertions produced under earlier versions. SDK support continues to evolve, and further extensions are in draft, so check cawg.io for the latest.

Vocabulary

  • Named actor: the person or organisation the assertion is about.
  • Credential holder: the party that controls the credential (key or verified identity) used to sign. Usually the same as the named actor.
  • Identity assertion generator: the software that helps the credential holder produce the assertion (often built into the same app as the claim generator).

Structure

An identity assertion has the label cawg.identity (multiple instances are allowed, such as cawg.identity__1, one per named actor). Its CBOR schema, simplified, is:

identity = {
  "signer_payload": {                         ; ← what the credential holder signs
    "referenced_assertions": [ hashed-uri, … ],  ; required: which assertions they vouch for
    "sig_type": "cawg.x509.cose"               ; required: kind of credential/signature
              / "cawg.identity_claims_aggregation",
    ? "role": [ "cawg.creator", … ]              ; optional: their relationship to the asset
  },
  "signature": bstr,   ; the credential holder's signature over signer_payload
  "pad1": bstr,        ; zero bytes, used to reserve space
  ? "pad2": bstr
}

Referenced assertions: what the named actor vouches for

referenced_assertions is a list of hashed URIs, the same {url, alg, hash} structure the claim uses. The credential holder chooses which assertions they’re putting their name to: perhaps the actions and some metadata, but not a location they don’t want to vouch for. Two rules are essential:

  • Every referenced assertion must match, by URL, alg and hash, an entry in the C2PA claim the identity assertion appears in (or in an ingredient’s claim). Otherwise: cawg.identity.assertion.mismatch.
  • The list must include the hard-binding assertion. Otherwise: cawg.identity.hard_binding_missing. Without it, the named actor’s signature wouldn’t be tied to any particular content and could be copied onto anything.

Roles

The optional role array states how the named actor relates to the asset. Defined values are cawg.creator, cawg.contributor, cawg.editor, cawg.producer, cawg.publisher, cawg.sponsor and cawg.translator. Custom roles are possible too.

Why the padding?

There’s a sequencing puzzle. The identity assertion is inside the claim, so it must exist before the claim is signed. But its contents (especially the signature) have a size that isn’t known until signing happens, perhaps remotely on the creator’s device. The generator reserves space up front and fills the remainder with zero bytes (pad1, pad2), so the final assertion fits exactly. Validators check the padding really is all zeros (cawg.identity.pad.invalid), so it can’t be used to smuggle data.

Two signatures, tightly woven

credential holder (Jane) claim generator (PhotoApp) │ │ signs signer_payload ───┐ │ (refs: hard binding, │ cawg.identity assertion │ actions, …; role) └──▶ [payload + signature] ──hash──▶ claim.created_assertions │ signs the claim (COSE)
  • Jane’s signature covers the hashes of the assertions she referenced, including the hard binding, so it covers the content.
  • PhotoApp’s claim lists the hash of the identity assertion, so the claim signature also covers Jane’s signature.
  • Changing anything breaks at least one signature. Copying Jane’s assertion into another manifest fails because her referenced hashes won’t appear in the new claim.

Try it

Jane Photographer has a certificate from an identity CA, separate from the C2PA trust list. PhotoApp is the claim generator. Choose what Jane vouches for and her role, sign, then attack.

Experiments:

  • Untick the hard binding and re-sign → cawg.identity.hard_binding_missing.
  • “Tamper with role”: Jane’s signature fails (cawg.x509.signature.mismatch), and so does the claim’s hashed URI for cawg.identity. Two independent alarms.
  • “Reuse on another photo”: the new manifest’s C2PA signature is perfect, but Jane’s referenced hard binding isn’t in the new claim → cawg.identity.assertion.mismatch.
  • Untick “Validator trusts Example Identity CA”. The signature still verifies, but the chain has no trust anchor → cawg.x509.credential.untrusted, so cawg.identity.trusted can’t be issued.

Credential type 1: X.509 certificates (cawg.x509.cose)

The simplest option, and the one the demo uses. The credential holder has an X.509 certificate and private key, just like a claim generator. The signature field holds a COSE_Sign1 signature over the CBOR-encoded signer_payload, with the certificate chain in its header and optionally a time-stamp. Validation follows C2PA’s signature, time-stamp and credential-checking procedure, adapted for the identity assertion. Since 1.3 it reports its own codes: cawg.x509.signature.validated / cawg.x509.signature.mismatch, cawg.x509.credential.trusted / cawg.x509.credential.untrusted, cawg.x509.time_stamp.*, cawg.x509.ocsp.* and so on.

Which certificates count as “trusted” for identity? Since 1.2, the spec defines a trust model. A validator maintains accepted Extended Key Usages (which must include id-kp-documentSigning), accepted certificate policies, and trust anchors. There is also an interim provision, for assertions generated on or before 31 March 2027, that accepts certain organisation-validated S/MIME-style certificates. This suits organisations, such as news agencies, that already run PKI or can buy certificates.

Credential type 2: identity claims aggregation (cawg.identity_claims_aggregation)

Most individual creators don’t have X.509 certificates. They have verified social media accounts, a personal website, perhaps an ID-verified account with some service. These signals have problems as lasting evidence: each platform presents them differently, they can’t sign anything on the person’s behalf, and they’re designed for checking now, not for checking a photo’s credit five years later.

Identity claims aggregation (ICA) solves this with a trusted intermediary, the identity claims aggregator. It:

  1. verifies the creator’s identity signals (logs in to their social accounts, checks their website, runs a document check…), and
  2. when the creator makes content, issues a W3C Verifiable Credential restating those verified identities and binding them to this asset’s signer_payload, signed with the aggregator’s own key.

Simplified, the credential looks like this:

{
  "@context": ["https://www.w3.org/ns/credentials/v2", "https://cawg.io/identity/1.1/ica/context/"],
  "type": ["VerifiableCredential", "IdentityClaimsAggregationCredential"],
  "issuer": "did:web:aggregator.example",
  "validFrom": "2026-05-01T12:00:00Z",
  "credentialSubject": {
    "id": "did:example:jane",
    "verifiedIdentities": [
      { "type": "cawg.social_media", "username": "janephoto", "uri": "https://social.example/@janephoto",
        "provider": { "id": "https://social.example", "name": "Social Example" }, "verifiedAt": "2026-04-02T09:00:00Z" },
      { "type": "cawg.web_site", "uri": "https://janephotographer.example", "verifiedAt": "2026-04-02T09:05:00Z",
        "provider": { "id": "https://aggregator.example", "name": "Aggregator" } },
      { "type": "cawg.document_verification", "name": "Jane Q. Photographer", "verifiedAt": "2026-03-30T14:00:00Z",
        "provider": { "id": "https://idv.example", "name": "ID Vendor" } }
    ],
    "c2paAsset": { "referenced_assertions": [ … ], "sig_type": "cawg.identity_claims_aggregation", "role": ["cawg.creator"] }
  }
}

Defined verifiedIdentities types include:

  • cawg.document_verification: government ID was checked (requires name);
  • cawg.web_site: control of a web site was verified (requires uri);
  • cawg.affiliation: membership of an organisation;
  • cawg.social_media: control of a social account (requires username);
  • cawg.crypto_wallet: control of a blockchain address (requires address).

DIDs (Decentralized Identifiers, such as did:web:aggregator.example) are a W3C standard for identifiers that resolve to a document listing public keys. A validator resolves the aggregator’s DID to find the key that should verify the credential. If the DID can’t be resolved, uses an unsupported method, or the signature fails, ICA-specific codes report it (cawg.ica.did_unavailable, cawg.ica.signature_mismatch…). Trust then rests on whether the validator trusts that aggregator (cawg.ica.untrusted_issuer otherwise).

Validating an identity assertion

In outline, a validator:

  1. Checks the C2PA manifest itself (chapter 8). The identity assertion is only meaningful inside a valid manifest.
  2. Parses cawg.identity (cawg.identity.cbor.invalid if malformed) and checks the padding.
  3. Checks every referenced assertion appears in the claim (cawg.identity.assertion.mismatch), that none is duplicated (cawg.identity.assertion.duplicate), and that the hard binding is included (cawg.identity.hard_binding_missing).
  4. Dispatches on sig_type (cawg.identity.sig_type.unknown if unrecognised) and verifies the credential-specific signature, time-stamp and revocation (cawg.identity.credential_revoked…).
  5. Makes a trust decision about the named actor:
    • cawg.identity.trusted: everything validated, and the named actor is trusted through a root of trust the consumer accepts.
    • cawg.identity.well-formed: everything validated, but no root of trust could be identified for the named actor. For X.509 credentials, 1.3 treats a chain that can’t be verified as a failure (cawg.x509.credential.untrusted), so you’re more likely to see this outcome with other credential types.
Key idea

The identity assertion is a second, independent signature from the person, woven into the product’s signature. Each covers the other, and both reach the content through the hard binding. Remove the identity assertion and you have an ordinary C2PA manifest. Forge it and the cryptography catches you.

Naming rule

The CAWG spec notes that “Content Credential” is a trademarked term referring to C2PA manifests, and it must not be used for identity assertion signatures. It’s a small detail, but a sign of how carefully the two layers are kept distinct.
