Time-stamps and the long life of a signature

Certificates expire after a year or two. Photos are meant to stay credible for decades. A trusted time-stamp bridges that gap.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to

  • Explain why a signature without a trusted time-stamp becomes unverifiable once its certificate expires.
  • Describe what an RFC 3161 Time-Stamp Authority signs, and what it never sees.
  • Explain how time-stamps interact with revocation.

The expiry problem

Certificates have short lifetimes on purpose: the shorter a key is in use, the less damage a leak can do. But think about what a validator must ask when checking a C2PA manifest years later:

“Was the signer’s certificate valid at the moment this signature was made?”

The validator can’t take the signer’s word for the time. Any timestamp the signer writes into the data was chosen by the signer. Someone who stole an expired key could simply write an old date. Without independent proof of when, the only safe choice is to check the certificate against now. Once the certificate expires, the signature becomes unverifiable.

Time-Stamp Authorities

A Time-Stamp Authority (TSA) is a trusted third party with an accurate clock and its own certificate (with the timeStamping extended key usage). The protocol, defined in RFC 3161, is simple:

  1. The signer computes a hash of their signature, called the message imprint, and sends only that hash to the TSA.
  2. The TSA builds a time-stamp token containing the imprint and its current time (genTime), and signs it with its private key.
  3. The signer embeds the token alongside their signature.

A validator later checks that:

  • the TSA’s signature on the token is valid and its certificate chains to a trusted TSA root, and
  • the imprint in the token equals the hash of this signature.

If both hold, the validator knows the signature existed no later than genTime. It then checks the signer’s certificate against genTime instead of “now”. The TSA never sees the photo, the manifest or the private key. It only sees a 32-byte hash.

Things to try:

  • With the time-stamp on, drag “validated” far into the future. It stays valid.
  • Turn the time-stamp off. The signature fails as soon as the validation date passes the certificate’s expiry.
  • Turn on revocation. With a time-stamp, signatures made before the revocation date remain acceptable, and those made after are rejected. Without one, the validator can’t tell, so it rejects them all after revocation.
  • Move “signed” before the certificate’s start date. Rejected either way.
A subtlety about revocation

Real-world policy is a bit more nuanced. A revocation for “key compromise” may record an estimated compromise date, and validators may apply different policies to different revocation reasons. The principle the demo shows, that a trusted time-stamp lets you separate signatures made before a problem from those made after, is the important part.

Time-stamps in C2PA

C2PA recommends that claim generators obtain an RFC 3161 time-stamp for every claim signature and embed it in the COSE signature structure. Validators report outcomes such as:

  • timeStamp.validated: the token is intact and covers this signature.
  • timeStamp.trusted: the TSA chains to a trusted TSA root.
  • timeStamp.mismatch: the token does not match this signature.
  • claimSignature.insideValidity / claimSignature.outsideValidity: whether the signing certificate was valid at the established signing time.

One more detail you’ll exploit in the Tamper Lab. In COSE, the time-stamp is placed in the unprotected header, outside the bytes the claim generator signs. Removing it therefore doesn’t break the claim signature. It just takes away the proof of time, which only matters once the certificate has expired.
