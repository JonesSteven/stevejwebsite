Ingredients and provenance chains

Real content passes through many hands: a camera, an editor, a compositing tool, a publisher. C2PA records each step as its own signed manifest, linked to the previous ones.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to

  • Explain how ingredient assertions link manifests into a provenance graph.
  • Distinguish parentOf, componentOf and inputTo relationships.
  • Explain redaction, and why C2PA lets you remove some information.
  • Explain metadata stripping, and how soft bindings and manifest repositories help.

Every tool signs only its own step

A key design rule: a claim generator never alters or re-signs another generator’s manifest. When PhotoApp edits a photo from a camera, it can’t sign on the camera’s behalf, and it doesn’t need to. Instead it:

  1. keeps the camera’s manifest, unchanged, in the manifest store;
  2. validates it, and records the result;
  3. adds an ingredient assertion pointing to that manifest with its hash;
  4. adds an actions assertion (c2pa.opened the ingredient, then whatever edits were made);
  5. adds a hard binding for the new content, and signs its own claim.

The new manifest becomes the active manifest. The result is a graph: the active manifest at the top, with arrows down to its ingredients’ manifests, and from them to their ingredients, back to the moment of capture or creation.

Each hard binding describes its own moment

There’s an obvious puzzle here. The camera’s manifest contains a hard binding: the hash of the pixels it captured. PhotoApp then changes those pixels. Surely the camera’s hard binding no longer matches, and the file should fail validation?

It would, if validators checked it against the current file, but they don’t. Each manifest’s hard binding describes the content as it was when that manifest was signed. Validation treats the two kinds of manifest differently:

  • The active manifest (the newest one) is checked completely, including its hard binding against the bytes you are holding. That is what proves this file is exactly what its last signer produced.
  • Ingredient manifests are checked for everything except their hard binding: signature, certificate chain, time-stamp, and the hashes of their assertions. The C2PA specification says so explicitly: hard binding assertions “cannot be validated for ingredients”, because the earlier version of the content is no longer there to hash.

So who checked the camera’s hard binding? PhotoApp did, at the moment it opened the camera’s file. At that point the original pixels were still available. PhotoApp validated the camera manifest completely and wrote the outcome into the ingredient assertion’s validationResults. It also recorded the hash of the camera’s manifest, so nobody can later swap in a different one. A later validator re-checks everything it still can, and compares its findings with the recorded results.

The chain therefore holds together link by link. The camera vouches for the capture. PhotoApp vouches that it opened a file carrying that valid camera manifest, and for the edited pixels it produced. The active manifest’s hard binding proves you hold exactly those pixels.

Relationships

  • parentOf: the ingredient is the asset being edited. There can be at most one parent.
  • componentOf: the ingredient was placed into the asset (a logo, a second photo, an AI-generated element).
  • inputTo: the ingredient was used to produce the asset without being part of it, for example a reference image given to an AI model.

Following the componentOf link shows the sky’s own manifest, signed by the AI service, with digitalSourceType: trainedAlgorithmicMedia. Following parentOf leads back through the edit to the camera capture. Each link carries a hash, so a validator can confirm that every ingredient manifest is exactly the one that was referenced.

This is what makes C2PA useful for transparency about AI. A composite can be honest about which parts are AI-generated, and the claim comes from the AI service itself, not just from whoever made the final image.

Could an editor lie about what it did?

Yes. A tool could crop a photo and say “cropped”. It could just as easily paint a fire onto a mountain, or substitute a different photo altogether, and still say only “cropped”. Every cryptographic check would pass. The editor’s signature is genuine, its hard binding matches the pixels it produced, and the camera’s manifest is untouched. Turn on the dishonest option in the demo above to see it.

This is a fundamental limit, not a bug. The actions assertion is a statement made by the signer, and a signature proves who made a statement, not that the statement is true. The specification is explicit that assertions in a claim are attributed to its signer, because the trust model is rooted in trust in the signer. Nothing in the file can prove what happened inside someone else’s software. What C2PA offers instead is a set of defences:

  • The lie is signed. The false claim is permanently attributable to the editor’s certificate. A lie can’t be anonymous and can’t later be denied.
  • Trust lists and conformance. Validators only show a signer as trusted if its certificate chains to the C2PA trust list. Generator products get those certificates through the C2PA conformance program. A product caught misrepresenting edits can have its certificate revoked (chapter 5). In the program’s first phase, conformance is largely self-asserted, so this is a deterrent rather than a guarantee.
  • Thumbnails. Manifests can carry signed thumbnails of the content as it was at each step (c2pa.thumbnail.claim, c2pa.thumbnail.ingredient). A viewer such as the Verify tool can show the camera’s thumbnail next to the image you’re looking at. A “crop” that added a fire, or replaced the scene, is then plain to see. The graph in the demo shows these thumbnails.
  • The original still exists. The camera’s manifest records the hash of the original capture. Whoever holds that file, such as the photographer or a newsroom archive, can prove what was really captured, and that the published version differs.
  • Your own policy. You decide which signers you believe. A newsroom might accept edits only from its own tools, or insist on the original capture for anything contentious.
The weakest link sets the trust level

A provenance chain is only as trustworthy as its least trustworthy signer. A genuine camera capture followed by an edit from an unknown tool tells you that the original was real and that someone changed it. It does not tell you that the change was as minor as claimed. Read every link with the question “do I trust this signer to describe its step honestly?”

Redaction: removing information on purpose

Sometimes earlier provenance contains something that shouldn’t travel further: the GPS coordinates of a journalist’s safe house, or a source’s name. Because the earlier manifest is signed, you can’t edit it. But C2PA allows a later claim generator to redact specific assertions from ingredient manifests:

  • The assertion’s contents are removed from the ingredient’s manifest (its box is zeroed or dropped).
  • The new claim lists the removed assertion in redacted_assertions and records a c2pa.redacted action.
  • Validators then know the gap is deliberate and accounted for. They won’t report the missing assertion as tampering, and they can show that something was redacted, and by whom.

Hard bindings can’t be redacted, since that would sever the link to the content.

The stripping problem

As the demo showed, if a platform strips metadata, the manifest store goes with it. Nothing inside the file is left to say there ever were credentials. This is the most significant practical limitation of any embedded-provenance system. C2PA attacks it from several directions:

  • Adoption by platforms: preserving (and adding to) Content Credentials instead of stripping them.
  • Soft bindings: a c2pa.soft-binding assertion records a watermark or fingerprint. If the manifest is lost, the watermark can still be read from the pixels, or the fingerprint recomputed, and used to look up the manifest.
  • Manifest repositories: cloud services where manifests are stored and can be found by soft-binding lookup.
  • Remote manifests: the asset may carry only a URL to its manifest store, which is smaller and more likely to survive.
Recovered ≠ byte-exact

A recovered manifest describes the asset as it was when signed. If the file has since been re-compressed, its hard binding won’t match the bytes you hold, so you’ve learned the history of a closely related original, not proven these exact bytes. Soft bindings trade some certainty for durability.

Ingredients without credentials

An ingredient may have no manifest at all: an old photo, a stock image without credentials. It is still recorded (title, format, maybe a thumbnail and a hash), but its provenance is unknown. The chain honestly stops there. Partial provenance is still far better than none, as long as the viewer can see where the verifiable part ends.
